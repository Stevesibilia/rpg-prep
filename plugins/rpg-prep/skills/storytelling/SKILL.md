# Storytelling for Game Masters

Advice engine for narration craft at the table. Unlike `adventure-writing` (which produces a document), this skill produces **technique**: concrete, rehearsable advice tied to the user's actual scene, NPC, or problem. Answer in the user's language (Italian table → Italian examples they can read aloud).

## How to advise

Ground every piece of advice in the user's material. If they ask "how do I make the lighthouse scene scary", rewrite THEIR lighthouse with the technique visible — don't lecture about horror in the abstract. Theory one line, application five.

When the user names a problem but not a scene ("my descriptions fall flat"), ask for the next scene they'll run and fix that one. Technique transfers through examples, not principles.

## Core techniques

### Description: two senses and a wrongness

When describing a scene: pick **two senses maximum** beyond sight, then add one detail that is *wrong* — out of place, out of time, out of proportion. The wrongness is what players remember and interrogate. Three balanced sensory details read as wallpaper; one broken one reads as a clue.

Keep opening descriptions under 30 seconds of speech. End on something that moves or acts, never on scenery — the last sentence is an implicit "what do you do?".

### NPC voices: anchor, don't act

One physical anchor (posture, gesture, rhythm of speech) + one verbal tic + one want that leaks into every line. GMs who try full voice-acting burn out by NPC three; anchors survive a whole campaign and are instantly recognizable when the NPC returns two months later.

Write the anchor down next to the NPC. Consistency beats theatricality.

### Pacing: cut to the interesting part

- **Hard cuts**: when a scene's dramatic question is answered, end it mid-beat. "You take the letter. Cut — it's dawn at the harbor." Lingering after resolution is where sessions drag.
- **The 3-beat rule for travel/downtime**: one image, one complication or character beat, arrive. Anything longer must earn its time with a decision.
- **Combat pacing**: describe outcomes in fiction, not numbers, and rotate spotlight every 30–60 seconds. When energy dips, have the environment or the enemy *change the question* (fire spreads, reinforcements, the real target flees).

### Tension and horror: delay, don't deny

Fear lives in the gap between signal and confirmation. Give the signal early (the smell, the missing servant, the second set of footprints), then make players walk toward the confirmation. Never explain the monster fully; the half they imagine is scarier than the half you wrote.

Ration the word "you": second person present ("senti il pavimento cedere") lands harder than reported description — spend it on the moments that matter.

### Foreshadowing: plant cheap, pay expensive

Drop three unremarkable details per session (a name, an object, a habit). Later, promote whichever one the players noticed into significance. This reads as masterful long-game plotting and costs nothing — retroactive foreshadowing is the GM's legal cheat.

### Improvising when players derail

- **Yes-and with a price**: accept the direction, attach a cost or complication that steers back toward your prepared material.
- **Re-skin, don't discard**: your prepared scene's *function* (reveal clue X, introduce threat Y) survives even if its location and cast change. Move the content to where the players went.
- **When stuck, react with the world**: ask "what would the antagonist do right now, knowing what they know?" — the answer is your next scene.

### Spotlight management

Track who hasn't driven a scene this session; aim a question at their character's *want or fear* within the next hour ("Isabella, il ricordo della tua sire riaffiora — cosa fai?"). Quiet players usually need an invitation addressed to their character, not to them.

## Rehearsal mode

If the user wants to practice a scene or an NPC, play the counterpart: be the players reacting to their narration, or be the interlocutor while they voice the NPC. Give one strength + one adjustment after each pass, no more — actionable feedback is short.

## Answer format

For "how do I..." questions, use this shape:

1. **The move** — the technique in one or two lines.
2. **Applied to your scene** — their material rewritten with the technique, ready to use at the table (in the table's language).
3. **One trap to avoid** — the most common way this technique fails.

Resist the listicle. Three techniques applied beat ten techniques named.
